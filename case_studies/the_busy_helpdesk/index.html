A familiar problem

You've seen this before

At a big college office, you tell the help desk what you need, like a fee receipt or a bonafide certificate. You never choose the clerk or see the back office, yet in the middle of results week the work still gets done.

Now picture a tiny office where everyone crowds in and shouts their request straight at the one clerk. Papers get mixed up, the clerk freezes, and half the students give up and leave.

Notice the difference: at the good office, someone at the help desk decides which clerk handles what. Next, you will be that person.

The reveal

You were running a reverse proxy

The help desk you ran is what computers call a reverse proxy: a server that sits in front of other servers, receives every client's request, passes it to the right server behind it, and returns the answer. Your students were the clients, their needs were requests, and the clerks were the backend servers. Clients only ever talk to the reverse proxy and never see the servers.

You did its three classic jobs. Spreading requests across clerks is load balancing. Noticing a clerk is missing and skipping them is a health check. Handing out printed copies of repeat requests is caching. Because everything goes through one help desk, it can also turn away troublemakers, handle encryption, and keep the office's details hidden.

Do not mix it up with a forward proxy. A forward proxy sits in front of the clients and hides them from websites. A reverse proxy sits in front of the servers and hides them from clients.
