Chapter 1 · A familiar problem

You've reached for the top one before

At a cafeteria, clean plates are placed one on top of another. When you need a plate, you take the one on top, not the one at the bottom. To reach a plate lower down, you would have to lift off every plate above it first.

The same thing happens with a pile of notebooks on a desk: the last notebook you put down is the easiest one to pick up again. When you can only reach the top, the order you added things decides the order you can take them back.

Now imagine you put down a red notebook, then a blue one, then a green one. You want the red notebook again. Which ones do you have to pick up first, and why can't you grab the red one right away?

Chapter 2 · How to play

Before You Browse

Goal: build a visit pile and notice which page you return to when you go back.

  • Use + Visit Page to open a new page. It lands on top of your visit pile.
  • Use Back (or the browser back arrow) to return. It removes the top page, the one you saw last.
  • Try Auto Surf to watch a full run, or use the Challenge to reach a target page in a set number of Backs.
  • No jumping: you cannot open a middle page directly, and you cannot search old pages from the pile.
  • Visiting a new page after going back clears the pages that were ahead of you.

Chapter 5 · The Method

What you just discovered

The visit pile you used in the browser has a name.

The visit pile worked because every new page was placed on top, and Back always removed the top page. The page you saw most recently was the first one you returned to. You could not reach the middle without removing what was above it.

In computer science, this is called a stack. A stack follows one simple rule: Last In, First Out (LIFO), the last item you put in is the first item you take out.

  • Push, add a new item to the top of the stack.
  • Pop, remove the item from the top of the stack.
  • Because you only touch the top, adding and removing stay quick, even when the pile is large.

That is why the Back button feels natural: it returns you to the most recent page first. When you visited a new page after going back, the old forward path disappeared, because the top of the pile changed.

Key learnings

1.
Only the top is reachable

You add to the top and remove from the top. The middle stays out of reach.

2.
Last in, first out

The most recent item is the first one to come back out.

3.
The rule shapes the behavior

Because Back always removes the newest page, your steps retrace in reverse order.

Chapter 6 · New Cases

Try on your own

Same idea, new situations. Think each one through.

01 · Number the stepsA photo app records screens you open in this order: Gallery, Album, Photo, Edit. You tap Back once, open Share, then tap Back twice. Which screen is showing now, and in what order did the screens you left get removed? (Trace it step by step.)
02 · Undo in an editorYou type A, then B, then C. You press Undo twice. Which letters disappear first, and why does Undo follow the same idea as a browser's Back button?
03 · How many steps back?You have opened 15 screens in a row and now want the screen you opened 9 steps before the current one. If you can only go back one step at a time, how many Back actions do you need, and how many screens do you pass by on the way? Could a single jump be done if only the most recent screen is remembered?
04 · Nothing to undoA drawing app has no actions left to undo, but the user clicks Undo. What should the app do, and why should a program check for this case before trying to remove the “last” action?
05 · A new path replaces the old oneIn a file manager you open Folder A, then B, then C, go Back twice to A, and then open Folder D. Should “Forward” still take you to B or C? Explain what information would be lost or kept, and why that choice makes sense to a user.
06 · Reaching the middleYou want to return to an item you handled several steps ago, but you must not lose the record of the steps you took after it. If you are only allowed to remove the most recent item first, what would you have to do? What does this reveal about when this structure is helpful and when it is limiting?
07 · Two tools, different rulesA browser's Back button returns you to the most recent page. A print queue prints the document that arrived first. Both handle a list of things, but in different orders. When is “most recent first” the right choice, and when is “first arrived first” better?
08 · Design a breadcrumb trailYou are designing a “Back” feature for a learning app with many screens. Which part of this problem fits a stack, and what would you store each time the user moves to a new screen so Back works correctly?
