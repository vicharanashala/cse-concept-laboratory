Chapter 1 · The Orders

🍕 Doing More With Less

Imagine you have several jobs to get done, but only a limited number of hands to do them. Some parts of a job take active effort, and other parts just need time to run on their own, like something baking, a file downloading, or water boiling.

If you sit and wait for each job to fully finish before starting the next one, you waste all the moments where nothing needed your attention. But if you juggle your attention across jobs, or bring in more hands to work alongside you, you can get more done.

Let's play a shift at a busy food truck and see exactly how that plays out. You'll be in charge of the kitchen.

Chapter 5 · The Method

How the two shifts really worked

You ran the same three orders two different ways. Here's what each strategy is called, and what makes them different.

That's exactly the habit you built by hand during the first rush:

  • Ravi starts a step, and if it needs the oven, grill, or blender, he moves to a different order instead of standing there.
  • He keeps circling back, checking what's ready and starting the next step.
  • Every order keeps inching forward, even though Ravi is only touching one thing at any instant.
  • The trick is using the waiting time on one task to make progress on another.

That behavior has a name: Concurrency. It means handling multiple tasks during the same period, without necessarily doing them at the exact same instant.

In the second rush, the strategy changed completely:

  • Ravi, Maya, and Arjun each took one order and stayed with it from start to finish.
  • All three were working at the same instant. Nobody had to switch dishes or wait for a turn.
  • The shift finished far sooner, because the work was split across three pairs of hands.

That is Parallelism. It means multiple tasks genuinely running at the same time, because there are multiple workers to run them.

⚖️ Concurrency vs Parallelism

🍳 Concurrency (Scenario 1)

  • One worker: Ravi.
  • Tasks take turns, switching whenever one is waiting.
  • Only one thing is being touched at any instant.
  • About managing many tasks at once.

⚡ Parallelism (Scenario 2)

  • Several workers: Ravi, Maya, and Arjun.
  • Each task has its own worker.
  • Many things are truly happening at the same instant.
  • About doing many tasks at once.

The two aren't rivals. A kitchen could have three cooks and let each one juggle several dishes, using both ideas together.

🔑 Key Learnings

🍳
Concurrency

One worker switches between tasks, using the waiting time on one to make progress on another.

⚡
Parallelism

Multiple workers act at the same time, each one truly executing their own task simultaneously.

✅
Why It Works

Waiting time, like something baking, doesn't have to be wasted time. Concurrency makes use of it with a single worker, and parallelism goes further by adding more workers.

🌍
Where You'll See It

Operating systems, web servers, multi-core processors, and everyday multitasking all lean on these same two ideas.

1 · Decision-makingCampus Bites receives six orders at once. The owner can assign one cook to handle tasks in turns or assign three cooks to prepare different orders simultaneously. 2 · Recognize the PatternA single support agent handles chats with three different customers, replying to each one while the others are typing. Is this closer to concurrency or parallelism? Explain your reasoning.
3 · Spot the MisconceptionSomeone says, "Concurrency just means using one CPU, and parallelism just means using more than one." What is wrong with this reasoning?
4 · Resource constraintsThree cooks are available, but the kitchen has only one oven. Which activities can the cooks perform concurrently while one order is baking? What would happen if all three orders needed the oven at the same time? Explain why adding more cooks might not reduce the total preparation time proportionally.
5 · Unfamiliar ApplicationA laptop with a single processor core appears to run a browser, a music player, and a chat app all "at once." How might concurrency explain what's really happening?

How would you organize these activities to finish all the orders as quickly as possible? Would adding more cooks always help? Explain your approach.

Would you handle all these tasks concurrently, execute some of them in parallel, or use a combination of both? How would you decide which approach offers the best performance?
