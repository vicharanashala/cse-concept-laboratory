Step 1 · The Problem

Squeezing tomorrow's lemons twice is expensive.

Maya runs a lemonade stand on the corner. Every evening she writes one line in a small notebook: that day's temperature, and how many cups she sold.

Tomorrow's forecast reads 31 degrees. Maya has to squeeze and ice the lemons tonight, so she needs a number before the day begins. Too few cups and she turns people away; too many and the batch goes warm.

She has sixteen past days, no formula, and no time for a guess. Her question is simple and hard at once:

Can the days she already lived through tell her how many cups tomorrow will bring - and how much should she trust that number?

Step 2 · The Story

Sixteen days, one straight line

Maya spreads her notebook across the counter. Each past day becomes a dot: hotter to the right, more cups higher up. She wants one simple rule that runs through them.

Every possible line gives a different answer for tomorrow. Maya does not want the prettiest line - she wants the line that is wrong by the smallest amount across every day she already lived through, because that is the line most likely to be right on a day she has not lived yet. One number decides how many lemons she squeezes tonight.

  • You control a line. Two sliders: slope tilts it, intercept lifts it up or down.
  • Every dot shows a dashed stick down to your line. That stick is the day's miss - how wrong the line would have been that day.
  • The score card averages the squared misses. Small is good; this number is the line's total embarrassment.
  • Bring the line onto the crowd of days, get the score as low as it will go, then let it predict tomorrow.
Step 3 · The Game

Bring the line onto the crowd

How to play

  1. Move the slope and intercept sliders and watch the orange line swing across the days.
  2. Make the dashed misses as short as you can. The score card shrinks when the line sits where the days actually are.
  3. Press Auto Fit to watch the line settle onto the best spot, then read tomorrow's 31 degree prediction.
  4. Then try to beat that score. Nudge a slider and watch it climb back up - that is how you know the fit you found is the lowest one.

Step 4 · Reflect

What did you notice?

No single right answer. Just what you saw on the stand.

01 · Days that pull hardestSome days sat right on your line, others sat far away. When you moved a slider, which kind of day seemed to drag the line the most?
02 · Two lines that look differentYou can find two quite different lines that give almost the same score. If both fit about as well, what would make one of them more worth trusting?
03 · A miss high, a miss lowOne day sells five cups more than the line predicts, another sells five cups fewer. If you simply added every miss together, those two would cancel out. Would that be an honest scorecard?
04 · The one odd dayMaya had one hot day with almost no sales. When you added it, the line leaned toward it. Was the line being fair, or was it being fooled?
05 · A day you have never seenTomorrow is hotter than any day in the notebook. How would you decide whether to trust the line's number for that day?
Step 5 · The Concept

Now name it

Linear regression finds the single straight line that makes the total squared distance from every data point to the line as small as possible, then uses that line to predict a value for a new input.

Why it matters

Maya has to commit to one answer before the day begins. The least-squares line is the one that, across every day she already lived through, is wrong by the smallest possible amount - no other straight line describes her own data better. A line that sits systematically high or low means lemons squeezed and thrown away, or customers turned away. Least wrong on the past is the most trustworthy bet on the future.

Mechanism

  1. Draw a candidate straight line through the data: prediction = slope × input + intercept.
  2. For each point, measure the vertical gap between the real value and the line's prediction - the residual.
  3. Square every residual so that overshoot and undershoot both count and large misses count more, then average them into one number, the mean squared error.
  4. Adjust the slope and intercept until no small change lowers that number. The line that remains is the least-squares fit.

Where the numbers come from

Least squares has a shortcut, so no trial and error is really needed:

  • slope = (how temperatures and cups rise together) / (how spread out the temperatures are). For Maya's sixteen days that is 727.5 / 340 = 2.14 - about two more cups for every extra degree.
  • intercept = average cups - slope × average temperature. That is 39.4 - 2.14 × 23.5 = -10.8, the number that lifts the line so it passes through the average day (23.5 degrees, 39.4 cups).

The negative intercept is not a real prediction. It is only the value the line would report at 0 degrees - far outside the days Maya actually recorded, and impossible for a lemonade stand. The line is only trusted across the range she lived through.

Vocabulary

feature
The input you start from - here, the day's temperature.
target
The value you want to predict - here, cups sold.
slope (weight)
How many extra cups each extra degree adds.
intercept (bias)
The line's value when the feature is zero - its baseline.
residual
The vertical miss between a real point and the line.
mean squared error
The average of the squared residuals; the number a fit tries to shrink.
Eyeballing it A line that looks about right
Least-squares fit The one line with the smallest average squared miss

Real situations where a fitted line helps, or quietly fails. For each one, explain your reasoning in your own words.
