Chapter 1 · The Puzzle

The records office has 10,000 cards and one afternoon.

Rhea works in the records office of a national statistics bureau. Over the years, births were written onto cards: a name, a date of birth. Today a researcher wants them in date order, and Rhea has one afternoon to do it.

The slow way is the way she already knows. Take a card, hold it against every other card, ask "which is earlier?", and swap. Do that to all of them. She has done the arithmetic in her head before: for one card you might look at thousands of others, and you do it ten thousand times.

Then her supervisor shows her the wall behind her desk. It is not a shelf, it is a filing cabinet, and every drawer is labelled with a range of years. "Put each card in the drawer that covers its year. You will never have to compare two cards from different drawers again. Only the cards inside the same drawer need lining up."

Rhea still has to open every drawer and order the cards within it. The question is: why is that faster, and when would it not be faster at all?

  • Read the drawer label to place a card. No comparing needed.
  • Only cards inside the same drawer ever get compared.
  • Compare every card against every other card, one at a time.
  • Sort the whole list again from scratch after each single swap.
Chapter 2 · The Briefing

Two passes, not one long fight

Every card shows a year. The drawers are labelled with equal ranges of years, so the label alone tells you which drawer a card belongs in. You never compare a card to a card to find its drawer.

Your two passes

  • Pass 1, Spread. Click a card so it lifts off the table, then click the drawer whose label covers that year. The card flies in and shows the drawer it landed in.
  • Pass 2, Order. Inside a drawer, click the card that is in the wrong spot, then click the card it belongs in front of. Or click the end strip to place it last. That is insertion sort: each card is slid into place among the cards already in its drawer.
  • Stuck? Press Hint and the right drawer or neighbour will light up.

If two cards show the same year, they can sit in either order. Twins happen.

Your mission

  • Finish all three shifts to see the full pattern, or skip ahead whenever you like.
  • Compare the saving. Each finished shift reports how much comparing the drawers saved you. Compare that number across the three shifts.
Filing = 1 hand step Ordering = 1 step per neighbour you look at Hint = free highlight

A card belongs in a drawer because its own year says so. The drawer label decides, never a comparison.

Chapter 4 · Case Notes

What did you notice?

No single right answer. Just what you saw in the vault.

01 · Filing without comparingTo put 1947 in a drawer, you never asked "is 1947 bigger than this other card?". What did you read instead, and what made that cheaper?
02 · Only one drawer to fightOnce the cards were filed, ordering never had to compare cards from two different drawers. Why is that already most of the work done?
03 · The fullest drawerLook at the busiest drawer in each shift. The cards inside it are the only ones still being compared, so which shift felt heaviest, and why?
04 · The saving figureThe completion panel reported a saving every time, but Shift 1 saved far more than Shift 3. Shift 3 even had more cards. What actually changed?
05 · Equal rangesAll the drawers covered the same span of years. What would happen if the ranges were uneven, so some drawers were far wider than others?
06 · Beyond the vaultWhere else do people sort by putting things into labelled ranges first: exam marks into grade bands, photos into year folders, parcels into weight classes?
Chapter 5 · The Method

A wall of drawers beats one long queue of comparisons

Rhea never "sorted faster". She changed the shape of the job. Here is the name.

🗄

Bucket Sort

Definition

Bucket sort spreads the values into buckets by range first, then orders each bucket, usually with insertion sort. Like the cabinet, a value finds its own drawer by reading its own range, so no value is ever compared against a value in another bucket.

Mechanism

  1. spread each value into its bucket by reading its own range. The formula bucket(v) = floor((v - min) / width) picks the drawer in one step, no comparisons.
  2. order each bucket with insertion sort: slide each card into place among the ones already in that drawer.
  3. concatenate the ordered buckets in range order: drawer one, then the next, and so on, into one sorted list.
  4. count the cost: one step per value to spread, plus the sorting that now happens only inside each bucket.

Vocabulary

bucket
One labelled range of values, the drawer a card reads its way into.
bucket width
How wide each labelled range is; equal widths keep every drawer short.
bucket distribution
How the values spread across buckets: even, or clumped into one.
insertion sort
The simple internal sort used to order each bucket.
O(n + k)
The cost when values spread evenly and k buckets keep each list short.
Compare everything n² / 2 Every card meets every other card. With 10,000 cards that is about 50 million looks.
Spread, then order n + k One step to find the drawer, then only the cards sharing a drawer get compared.

Key Learnings

📦
Range Beats Comparison

A value does not need to be measured against anything to find its drawer. It reads its own number and the label on the drawer, and that single step places it.

🚪
Only One Drawer to Fight

Once a card is filed, it is never compared against a card in another drawer again. Every remaining comparison is already local.

📊
Distribution Is the Whole Trick

Even spread means every drawer stays short and the savings are huge. Clumped values put almost everything in one drawer, and you are back to sorting one long list.

🏷️
Buckets Must Be Decided First

Bucket sort needs to know the range and the width before it starts. When those are unknown or the values are strings, another method wins.

Chapter 6 · New Cases

Put the method to work

Real situations where sorting by range first helps, or quietly fails. For each one, decide whether a bucket belongs, and why.

Try these on paper

01 · Bad bucketsYou sort exam marks with bucket sort. Most students score between 70 and 80. Which bucket does the work, and what does that do to your running time?
02 · Choosing widthValues run from 0 to 900 and you have 900 of them. If you make 10 buckets, how many values land in each, and is that the choice you would make?
03 · Inside the drawerWhy does it not matter much which sorting algorithm you use inside a bucket, as long as the values spread evenly?
04 · Counting insteadA friend says "if the values are whole numbers from 0 to 100, just count them". Which sort is that, and how is it different from what Rhea did?
05 · A drawer for every markA teacher draws one bucket for every mark, 0 right up to 99, but the class only ever scores between 78 and 82. How many of those drawers will ever hold a card, and what does drawing 95 empty drawers really cost the sort?
06 · Names not numbersBucket sort places a value by reading its own number. Which step breaks the moment you try it on last names, and what would you reach for instead?
07 · Two cards, one comparisonA drawer holding two cards needs one comparison between them; a drawer holding one needs none. If the data spreads so no drawer ever holds more than two, how many comparisons does the whole sort need, and why does that stay small even with ten thousand cards?
08 · The year that came lateA card is found after the drawers are labelled, with a year that falls between two existing drawers. Can bucket sort still place it without relabelling everything? What must bucket sort know before it starts that a comparison sort never has to know?
09 · Marks on the edgeAn exam mark of exactly 80 sits where two grade bands meet. Who decides which drawer owns it, and what goes wrong if two drawers follow different rules at that same line? How would a comparison sort avoid the argument entirely?
