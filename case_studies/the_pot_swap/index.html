Chapter 1 · The Puzzle

One patch, six pots, only two hands.

Rohan has a seed tray with 6 potted seedlings, all different heights: the shortest at 3, the tallest at 8. Right now they sit mixed up in the tray, lined up in six spots.

He wants the shortest seedling in the first spot, the tallest in the last spot. But he has a rule for himself: he may only lift two pots at a time and trade their spots: a swap. Each swap costs effort, so he wants as few swaps as possible.

One clever bit: the moment a seedling lands in the spot where it belongs for good, that spot is done. Trading that pot again would be wasted effort.

Chapter 2 · The Briefing

A Swap at a Time

He wants the pots in height order in the tray: shortest in spot 1, tallest in spot 6.

  • One move = swap the spots of any two pots.
  • You may swap the same pot many times, but every swap counts.
  • When a pot reaches its forever-spot, it locks, so leave it alone from then on.
  • Goal: all 6 spots locked using 5 swaps or fewer.

Chapter 5 · The Method

How it really worked

You kept locking the smallest into its final spot with a swap. Here's the name.

🌱

Selection Sort

Definition

Selection Sort: find the smallest of what's left, put it in its final spot, lock it, repeat. Each round you scan the remaining pots, take the shortest one, and place it where it will live from now on.

In the game you did this with a swap: the shortest unlocked pot traded spots with the first unlocked spot. Because every other pot was already in the right place or still waiting, the swap locked the smallest for good.

Mechanism

find-minimum: look through the remaining and remember the smallest; swap it into the front of the unsorted part so it locks. The "sorted prefix" grows by one each round, and every item moves at most once.

Vocabulary

pass: one scan of the remaining items, minimum: the smallest remaining item, swap: trade the spots of two items, sorted prefix: the neat part you've already built, O(n²): repeats n passes, each scanning up to n items.

Shelf: you slid jars into the gapOne jar in hand, neighbours shuffled sideways to make room, and each new jar moved into place among the others.
Tray: you swapped pots in placeTwo pots traded spots at once; nothing else brushed against anything. Locked pots never moved again.

🔑 Key Learnings

🔍
Scan Everything First

To be sure you found the smallest, you must peek at every remaining item, even ones already neat. That repeat scan is the work.

🔄
Minimal Swaps

Each item is placed exactly once, straight into its final spot, at most n−1 swaps, never misplacing a locked pot.

🧩
Where You'll See It

Picking the smallest mango first, arranging medals on a shelf, sorting cards by pulling the lowest first, handy when moving an item is the expensive part, so you want each move to be final.

Chapter 6 · New Cases

Try on your own

Work it out in your head.

01 · First swapTray reads 5, 8, 3, 7, 6, 4. Selection sort's first pass finds the minimum and swaps it into spot 1. Which pot locks first, and which two spots trade?
02 · Swap totalFor any tray of 6 pots, selection sort finishes in at most 5 swaps. Why can it never need a 6th swap, even though there are 6 spots?
03 · Selection vs shelfInsertion sort slides each new item sideways into a gap, like jars on a shelf. When is swapping pots in place, the selection sort way, a better move than all that sliding?
04 · Reverse orderTray reads 8, 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, perfect reverse order. How many swaps does selection sort need, and how many passes does it still scan through?
05 · Locked alreadyHalfway through, the pot in spot 4 already holds the right value. Does selection sort ever swap it? How can you tell, without touching it, that it is safe to leave alone?
06 · Why O(n²)Locking spot 1 scans all 6 pots, spot 2 scans the 5 that remain, and so on. If the tray doubled to 12 pots, why does the work grow by about four times instead of two?
