Real-World Puzzle

The Town Fountain is Flooding

You have been placed in charge of the municipal water grid. Two hillside reservoirs feed water into two neighborhood holding tanks. From those two tanks, the water flows down into the public plaza fountain.

Between the reservoirs and the fountain sit six adjustable valves. Four valves govern water flowing into the two holding tanks, and two valves govern water flowing from the holding tanks into the fountain.

Right now, the fountain is receiving the wrong amount of water. If the flow is too low, the fountains dry up. If the flow is too high, the plaza floods.

Here is the catch: turning a valve right at the fountain is not enough. If an intermediate tank runs dry or overflows, the whole system breaks down. How do you adjust six coupled valves so that the final fountain reaches the exact target flow?

How to play

Balancing the Connection Grid

Your goal: bring the fountain to the exact target flow by adjusting valves one decision at a time.

Here is how the water network operates:

  1. Forward Flow: Water starts at Reservoir A and Reservoir B, enters Tank 1 and Tank 2, and combines into the Fountain.
  2. Measure the Gap: Look at the fountain meter. It indicates whether flow is too low (shortage) or too high (overflow).
  3. Trace Backward: To fix the mismatch, work backward from the fountain.
    • First adjust the valves directly connected to the fountain.
    • Then trace backward to replenish or drain the intermediate holding tanks.
  4. First watch a guided demonstration where every adjustment is explained. Then take over and balance the grid yourself.

The reveal

You Were Doing Backpropagation

In multi-layer systems, information flows forward from input sources through intermediate stages to generate an output. When that output is wrong, we face the credit assignment problem: how much responsibility belongs to each intermediate connection?

Backpropagation is the algorithm that solves this. Instead of guessing connection settings at random, it starts at the final output error and works backward through the network. Using the chain rule of calculus, it calculates the exact rate of change (the gradient) for every single connection in a single sweep.

New Weight = Current Weight - (Learning Rate × Error Gradient)
Where Error Gradient = (Change in Final Error) ÷ (Change in Connection Weight), computed backward from the output to each earlier layer.
The Two Sweeps of Learning

Thousands of Trials
Tuning one connection at a time by trial and error requires re-running the entire system for every parameter. For real networks with millions of parameters, this is impossible.
