A case study
The Hungry Mouse
Mile 1 · A Familiar Problem

You've seen this before

A puppy tries sitting, jumping and barking. Only one of them earns a treat, and nobody explains which. A few days later, it sits the moment you reach into your pocket.

You start a new video game level with no guide. You try moves, fall into traps, restart, and slowly learn the safe route.

Notice the shape: nobody hands over the answer. You try something, see what happens, and adjust. And sometimes you must try an unknown move to find something better than what you already know.

Next stop, you'll guide a hungry mouse this way.

Mile 2 · Before You Start

How this stop works

A hungry mouse is dropped into an unfamiliar kitchen and must find the cheese. It doesn't know which moves work, so it works that out from what happens after each move.

🧀 Cheese (run ends)+20
🌰 🍎 💧 Seed, apple, water (run ends)+5, +12, +8
🍞 Crumb, once per run+1
🪤 Mousetrap (run ends)−10
🧱 Bump into a wall−1

Guidelines

  1. Press Automatic to watch the mouse figure things out on its own. Use Exploration and Speed to change how it behaves.
  2. Press Try it yourself any time, even while Automatic is running. You become a fresh mouse in a bigger, tougher kitchen: you see only nearby squares, have 60 moves per run, and some routes end in dead ends or traps.
  3. Your goal: score the maximum points. Each kitchen shows its perfect score.

Mile 4 · Debrief

What just happened?

Think back on the kitchen. Talk these through. No answer to click.

1

After several attempts, what changes did you notice in the mouse’s movement? What do you think caused those changes?

2

Imagine the mouse always chooses the move that worked best in the past. What opportunities might it miss?

3

Some moves give immediate rewards, while others may lead to a better reward later. How should the mouse decide which moves are worth taking?

4

The mouse receives penalties for hitting walls and falling into traps. How do these negative experiences help it make better decisions?

5

If the mouse reaches a good reward once by chance, is that enough to conclude it has learned the best route? What further evidence would you look for?

Mile 5 · The Concept

The formal picture

What you did has a name: reinforcement learning. An agent learns by trying actions, collecting rewards and penalties, and improving its choices over time.

Official definition

Reinforcement learning is learning what to do, that is, how to map situations to actions, so as to maximize a numerical reward signal. The learner is not told which actions to take, but must discover which actions yield the most reward by trying them.

Source: Sutton and Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction

Mile 6 · Checkpoint

Spot the pattern elsewhere

Same shape, different scenery. Talk through each one.

1
Exploration vs. Exploitation

A delivery robot knows a route that takes 15 minutes but discovers an untested shortcut. How should it balance exploring the shortcut with using its reliable route when delivery time is limited?

2
Evaluating Learning

A video recommendation algorithm performs well during training but poorly for users with different interests. What does this reveal about its learning, and how would you test its performance on new users?

3
Designing Feedback

A parcel-sorting robot receives feedback only after sorting an entire batch. When some parcels are misplaced, it struggles to identify its mistakes. How could its learning process help it identify and learn from these errors?

4
Delayed Rewards

A traffic-control agent reduces congestion at one intersection, but traffic builds up at nearby intersections later. How should it account for these delayed consequences when choosing traffic-light timings?

5
Credit Assignment

A warehouse robot performs 20 actions before receiving a reward. If the task succeeds or fails, how could it identify which earlier actions contributed to the outcome?

6
Learning with Limited Experience

A rescue robot trained in open buildings struggles in collapsed buildings with narrow passages and debris. Why might its training be insufficient, and how could it prepare for unfamiliar environments?

7
Comparing Learning Strategies

Two recommendation systems receive rewards based on user engagement. One recommends familiar content, while the other explores unfamiliar content. How would you compare their long-term performance fairly?

8
Reward Design

A warehouse robot earns 10 points for every package moved, regardless of its destination. It moves more packages over time, but many end up in the wrong locations while its reward increases. What does this reveal about its learning, and how should the reward system change?

9
Competing Objectives

A hospital scheduling agent is rewarded for reducing patient waiting times but begins overbooking doctors and reducing consultation quality. Why does this happen, and how could its learning objective balance waiting time, workload, and care quality?

10
Evaluating Training Progress

A game-playing agent improves against familiar opponents but performs poorly against unfamiliar ones. How would you evaluate its progress and improve its performance against new opponents?
